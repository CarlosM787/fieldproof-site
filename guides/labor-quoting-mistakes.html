8 labor quoting mistakes, and what each one costs you

One siding repair, quoted right at $1,616.88, then quoted with each mistake: what it looks like on the job, what it costs in dollars, and how to avoid it with a free tool.

Published November 14, 2026 · TalkEstimate · 9-minute read

The short answer. A labor quote comes out low when the hourly cost, the hours or what goes on top (contingency, overhead and markup) aren’t the real ones, or when what’s not included and the changes never get written down. On the example job, each of these mistakes costs between $107.25 and $214.50 on a $1,616.88 price.

The example job, quoted right

It’s the repair from the guide to pricing labor: replacing a 12 × 8 ft section of rotted siding with a lead and a helper. You pay the lead $28 an hour, and each billable hour costs you $42.20 (the quote uses a round $42); the helper costs you $24 an hour. Every cost and rate in the quote is an example, not a market rate.

For mistakes 1 to 5, the cost is how far the price drops; for 6, how far it falls short of the margin you wanted. For 7 and 8 the price doesn’t change: it’s work you do without charging for it. Don’t add them up: each one is figured on its own, against the right quote.

1. Pricing labor at the wage, not at what an hour costs you

What it looks like. You pay the lead $28 an hour, so the quote says $28 an hour. But that hour costs you more: on top of the wage you pay payroll taxes and workers’ comp, and you pay for hours nobody is billed for: holidays, rain days, shop time. In the example, with 1,664 of 2,080 paid hours billed, a billable hour of the lead costs $42.20, 50.7% more than the wage.

What it costs. The lead’s line drops from $378.00 to $252.00: $126.00 of labor is missing, and the quote comes out at $1,412.13, $204.75 less. The crew still costs you $594.00, so once the contingency is spent and your overhead share is paid, you’re left with $168.38 instead of $373.13, less than half. Over a year, billing the lead’s 1,664 hours at the wage brings in $46,592.00 of the $70,223.20 the lead costs you: $23,631.20 short, before overhead or profit.

How to avoid it. Keep two numbers for each person: what an hour costs you and what you charge. The cost comes from a year of pay, payroll taxes, workers’ comp and other costs, divided by the hours you actually bill; the labor cost per hour calculator works it out from your numbers. If you work the job yourself, give your own hours a cost too.

The 9% payroll tax and the $9 per $100 of pay for workers’ comp are examples: they vary by state and trade. Social Security and Medicare alone are 7.65% of pay (IRS); federal and state unemployment tax come on top (IRS), and workers’ comp rates are set per class code and state.

2. Quoting the hours you hope for, not honest hours

What it looks like. “We’ll knock it out in 7 hours.” That’s what it took on the best day you ever had on a wall like this: no rusted nails, no board that won’t fit. Your timesheets from the last few similar jobs say 9 hours each, and that’s what the example uses.

What it costs. At 7 hours each, labor drops from $594.00 to $462.00 and the quote comes out at $1,402.38, $214.50 less. When the job takes its 9 hours, that $132.00 gets paid anyway: it eats the whole $86.30 contingency on that quote and $45.70 of your profit on top. And if there’s rot behind the boards, nothing is left to cover it.

How to avoid it. Quote the hours you actually paid on similar jobs, not the hours of your best day. If part of the job is uncertain, put it in the contingency, where you can see it, not in optimistic hours. In the labor and job quote calculator each person gets a line with their own hours; print the breakdown and, when the job is done, check it against the timesheets.

3. Counting only the hours on the wall

What it looks like. You count the time tearing off and hanging siding and forget the rest: loading the truck, driving, setup, the supply-house run for the trim you were short, cleanup, the nail sweep and the dump run. The example’s 9 hours each already include all of that.

What it costs. Leave out just one hour each (say, loading and cleanup) and you quote 8: labor drops to $528.00 and the quote to $1,509.63, $107.25 less. It’s the same math the guide to pricing labor shows: 2 crew hours are $66.00 of labor but $107.25 on the quote, because the contingency, the overhead share and the markup all grow with them. The $66.00 still gets paid, out of your contingency or your profit.

How to avoid it. Run the same list on every quote: load, drive, set up, work, clean up, haul away. In the labor and job quote calculator, paid drive hours go in the crew hours and fuel goes in Travel: each one counted once.

4. No contingency, or a hidden one

What it looks like. The price assumes everything behind the old boards is fine. Or you add an hour here and an hour there “just in case”, and in the end nobody knows how much cushion there is or where it went.

What it costs. Without the 10% contingency, the quote comes out at $1,487.53, $129.35 less. If $99.50 of rot turns up (exactly what the 10% would have covered), it comes out of what you keep: $243.78 instead of $373.13. Hiding it in the hours doesn’t cover enough either: padding the hours by 10% (9.9 each) sets aside only $59.40, because it grows with the labor and not with the $401.00 of materials, travel and disposal. And padded hours are the first thing you shave when you want to win the job.

How to avoid it. Give the contingency its own line, as a percentage of the direct job cost. How big it should be depends on how much of the job you can see before you start, and only you know that. If the job goes to plan, it stays with you: the example’s gross profit goes from $522.38 to $621.88. The labor and job quote calculator has a contingency field and shows you both numbers.

5. Forgetting overhead

What it looks like. The price covers direct labor, materials and some profit, but not the truck, insurance, phone or software. Those costs don’t show up on any one job, but every job has to pay its share.

What it costs. Without the overhead share, the 30% markup goes on $1,094.50 and the quote comes out at $1,422.85, $194.03 less. The overhead still gets paid: after its $149.25 share, the job leaves you $179.10 (12.6% of the price) instead of $373.13 (23.1%).

How to avoid it. Get your rate from your books: last year’s overhead divided by last year’s direct job costs. In the markup vs margin guide, $24,000 ÷ $160,000 = 15%, the same 15% this example uses; use your own numbers. Then price on top of it: in the labor and job quote calculator, the “Price on top of overhead” box does that.

6. Confusing markup with margin

What it looks like. “I put 30% on it,” and you take it that you keep 30% of the price. With markup you don’t: the 30% is figured on the cost, and you keep less of the price.

What it costs. A 30% markup on $1,243.75 gives $1,616.88, and after overhead you keep 23.1% of the price, not 30%. If what you wanted was to keep 30% of the price, the math is $1,243.75 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = $1,776.79 (rounded up to the cent): $159.91 more. To get there with a markup you need 42.9%, not 30%.

How to avoid it. Decide which number is yours: the markup you add or the margin you want to keep. If it’s margin, price by margin or convert: markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin). The markup vs margin calculator shows you both from your numbers. If a 30% markup is what you chose, $1,616.88 is right; it’s only a mistake if you meant margin.

7. One price, with nothing about what’s not included

What it looks like. “Replace the siding on the back wall: $1,616.88.” That’s all. The customer assumes you’ll also paint the new boards to match the house, and that was never in your price.

What it costs. If you paint them to keep the peace, that’s 2 hours of the lead at $42 ($84.00) and a gallon of paint at $42.50 (the example price in the paint job estimating guide): $126.50 of cost that isn’t on any line. It comes out of the $373.13 the job leaves you, more than a third of it, and you keep $246.63.

How to avoid it. Write a “not included” line: “Not included: painting the new boards.” Better still, offer it as a separate line with its own price: with the same steps (contingency, overhead and a 30% markup), painting comes to $205.57. With line items, the customer sees what they’re paying for and can drop a line instead of asking you to drop your profit. The free estimate template has one line per task and a space for notes and terms.

8. Changes on a handshake, with no change order

What it looks like. “While you’re here, can you replace the corner board too?” You say yes, you do it, and nobody wrote down a price.

What it costs. Say it’s one more hour each, not counting the board: 2 crew hours, $66.00 of labor, as in mistake 3. With the same steps, that change is worth $107.25, and the job total goes to $1,724.13. With nothing in writing, you either eat the $66.00 or show up at the invoice with $107.25 the customer thought was a favor.

How to avoid it. Before you do the change, write it down: what it is, what it costs, the new total and the customer’s signature. The free change order template puts it on one page to sign, and the free invoice template bills the estimate and its change orders on one invoice. If your state has rules for home improvement contracts, follow them; this guide doesn’t replace them.

What this guide doesn’t cover

  • Sales tax, permits and licensing aren’t in this math. Add whatever applies where you work.
  • Your costs and your prices. Every hourly cost and percentage in this guide is an example; it won’t tell you what to pay or what to charge.
  • Legal or tax advice. This guide isn’t either. For your taxes and your state’s rules, ask your accountant or adviser.

Questions contractors ask

How much does labor cost per hour?

There’s no one number that fits everyone: it depends on what you pay, your state and your trade. What goes on the quote is what a billable hour costs you: pay, payroll taxes, workers’ comp and other yearly costs, divided by the hours you actually bill. In this guide, a $28 wage costs $42.20 per billable hour. Work out yours with the labor cost per hour calculator.

How do I calculate labor for a quote?

People × hours × what an hour costs you, with one line for each hourly cost. In the example: 1 × 9 × $42 + 1 × 9 × $24 = $594.00. Then add materials, travel and disposal, the contingency, your overhead share and your markup. The guide to pricing labor walks through it step by step.

Which of these mistakes costs the most?

On this siding repair, quoting the hours you hope for (7 each instead of 9) cuts the price by $214.50, and pricing labor at the wage cuts it by $204.75. On your jobs it depends on where your risk is, so run the math with your own numbers.

What if the customer asks for a discount?

A discount comes straight out of what you keep. 10% off the $1,616.88 quote is $161.69, and what’s left after overhead drops from $373.13 to $211.44: 43.3% less. If you need to lower the price, take a line of work out instead of taking it out of your profit.

Should I show the contingency to the customer?

That’s your call. A contingency line is easier to talk through than padded hours, and if it isn’t needed, it stays with you. What matters is that it’s in your math, where you can see it, and not hidden in the hours.

Does this guide tell me what to charge?

No. The hourly costs, percentages and prices in this guide are examples. Your cost, your overhead and your markup come from your own numbers; the free calculators do the math with them.
