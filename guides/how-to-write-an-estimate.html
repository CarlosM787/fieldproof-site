ESTIMATING GUIDE

How to write an estimate your customer can say yes to

Write down the job while you’re there, turn it into one line per task, price each line from your own costs, say what isn’t included and how long the price is good for, then make it easy to approve. One living room, every number shown.

Published November 10, 2026 · TalkEstimate · 6-minute read

The short answer. A good estimate says who and where, lists the work line by line with a quantity and a price for each, shows the total, says what is not included, and states the terms: how long the price is good for, the deposit and payment, and how changes are handled. Then it tells the customer how to say yes.

Step 1: write down the job while you’re there

Walk the job with the customer and write the scope as you go: each room or area, its size, its condition, and exactly what they asked for. A photo of anything unusual (water stains, cracked drywall, a door that sticks) saves an argument later. What you don’t write down now, you’ll guess at tonight.

Step 2: one line per task, with a quantity

“Paint living room — $860” is hard to say yes to, because the customer can’t see what they’re paying for. Split it into tasks, and give each one a quantity and a unit: square feet of wall, linear feet of baseboard, one door, one ceiling. Lines also make it easy to drop or add work without rewriting the estimate.

Step 3: price each line from your own costs

Each line’s price should come from what the work costs you, not from what the last contractor charged. Count the hours honestly, add the materials at what you pay, then your overhead and markup. The labor pricing guide and the paint job guide walk through it with every number shown; the labor and job quote calculator does the math.

Step 4: add the work that’s easy to forget

Moving furniture, protecting floors, masking, setup, cleanup, the dump run, the trip to buy materials. They are real hours, and if they aren’t on a line, they come out of your profit. Give them their own lines or build them into the task they belong to, but don’t leave them out.

Step 5: say what isn’t included, and what you assumed

One or two lines prevent most surprises: “Not included: wall repairs beyond small nail holes, moving the piano, painting closets.” If the price depends on something you couldn’t see, say so: “Assumes the drywall behind the old wallpaper is sound.”

Step 6: state the terms

  • How long the price is good for (for example, 30 days), so a customer who calls back in March doesn’t expect last fall’s price.
  • Deposit and payment: how much to schedule the work, and when the rest is due.
  • Changes: any change to the work is written down, priced and approved before it’s done.
  • Start window, if you give one, written as a window and not a promise you can’t keep.

Step 7: make it easy to say yes

Send it while the job is still fresh in the customer’s mind, the same day if you can. Say how to approve it: a signature, a reply, or an approval link they can tap on their phone. A clear “to approve, sign here” gets more yeses than a price with no next step.

Estimate, quote or bid?

People use these words differently from trade to trade and place to place, and many contractors use them interchangeably. Whatever you call it, say on the document whether the price is fixed for the work listed or may change, and how long it’s good for. If your state has rules for home improvement contracts, follow them; this guide doesn’t replace them.

Five ways estimates lose the job (or the profit)

1. One lump sum

The customer can’t compare it, can’t trim it, and can’t see why it costs what it costs.

2. No “not included” line

Every gap gets filled with the customer’s assumption, usually the expensive one for you.

3. No expiration

Materials go up; a price with no end date is a promise you didn’t mean to make.

4. Sending it days later

By then the customer has two other estimates, and yours is the one they have to remember.

5. Changes agreed out loud

“While you’re here…” is new work. Write it down, price it, and get a yes before you do it.
