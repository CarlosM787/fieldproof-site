    How the totals add up

    Every amount is one you type. The template only multiplies, adds and subtracts, in a fixed order, so you and your customer can check each line on the printout.

    Line total quantity × unit price (no quantity: the price you typed, a flat price)
    Added work every added line, added up
    Credits every credit line, added up
    This change order added work − credits
    New contract total original contract + earlier change orders + this change order

    Each line is rounded to the cent once. A credit is typed as a positive amount and taken off. A line with no price adds nothing.

    What goes on a change order, and why

    • Which job and which estimate. The original estimate number and date tie this change to the price the customer already said yes to.
    • Why it changed. One or two plain sentences: what you found once you opened it up, or what the customer asked for.
    • One line per change. Added work and credits on their own lines, so the customer sees what goes on and what comes off.
    • The new total. The original amount, any earlier change orders and this one, so nobody has to guess what the job costs now.
    • The schedule. If the change adds days, say how many.
    • Two signatures, before the work. Write every change down and get it signed before you do the work. A yes said out loud is easy to remember differently later.

    The example, checked

    The example is change order #1 on the bathroom ceiling job from the free estimate template: estimate #1001, $854.40. Every price is an example, not a market rate. Four lines add work. Two use a quantity: 2 hr × $62.00 = $124.00 and 1 lot × $28.60 = $28.60. Two are flat prices: $140.00 for the extra drywall and $85.00 for the vanity light. That’s $377.60 of added work. One line is a credit: $42.00 for the ceiling paint the customer already has.

    So this change order is $377.60 − $42.00 = $335.60, and the new contract total is $854.40 + $335.60 = $1,190.00. It adds 1 day to the schedule.

    Questions contractors ask

    What is a change order?

    A written, priced change to a job the customer already approved: work added, work taken off (a credit), or both. It says why the work changed and what the job costs now, and it’s signed before the changed work is done.

    Can a change order lower the price?

    Yes. Pick Credit on a line for work that comes off the job or something the customer supplies, and type the amount without a minus sign. Credits come off the added work. If they’re bigger, this change order is a credit and the new contract total goes down.

    What goes in “earlier change orders”?

    The net of every change order already signed on this job, so the new contract total is right. Leave it blank on the first change order. If the earlier ones took more off than they added, pick “Took off the price.”

    Does it add sales tax?

    No. Every amount is the one you type, with no tax added. Whether labor, materials or both are taxable depends on your state and the kind of job. Your state’s rules (or your accountant) decide whether you add any tax line.

    How do I save it as a PDF?

    Press Print or save PDF and choose “Save as PDF” as the printer. On a phone, look under Print or Share. Then send the PDF to your customer by email or text.

    Can I print a blank one to fill in by hand?

    Yes. Leave the lines empty and press Print or save PDF: everything you left empty prints as a blank line to write on. Fill in only your business first and you get your own blank change order form.

    Are the example terms legal advice?

    No. They’re terms contractors commonly use, not legal advice. If your state has rules for home improvement contracts, follow them; this template doesn’t replace them.

    Where do the numbers go?

    Nowhere. Everything you type stays on this page, in your browser, and nothing is sent to us. It isn’t saved either, so print or save the PDF before you close the page. On talkestimate.com we count that the template was used, never what you typed.
